What is a PID controller?

A PID controller (Proportional–Integral–Derivative controller) is a feedback-control algorithm that continuously computes an error — the difference between a desired setpoint and a measured process variable — and applies a correction built from three terms:

  • P (Proportional) — reacts to the current error. Bigger error, bigger push.
  • I (Integral) — reacts to accumulated past error, eliminating steady-state offset.
  • D (Derivative) — reacts to the rate of change of the error, damping oscillation and predicting overshoot before it happens.

Overshoot, oscillation, and steady-state error

Overshoot happens when the controller pushes the process variable past the setpoint before it settles — usually from too much proportional or integral gain, or too little damping. Oscillation is repeated overshoot/undershoot around the setpoint; if it never dies out, the loop is unstable. Steady-state error is a persistent gap between setpoint and measurement even after transients settle — proportional-only control almost always leaves some, which is precisely what the integral term is designed to remove.

Common pitfalls

  • Integral windup — the integral term keeps growing while the actuator is saturated (at a physical limit), causing large overshoot once the error condition ends. Fixed by clamping the integral accumulator or the output (anti-windup), as shown above.
  • Derivative kick — a sudden setpoint change causes a huge spike in the derivative term if it's computed from the error. This page's controllers use derivative-on-measurement by default to avoid this.
  • Noisy derivative — differentiating noisy input amplifies the noise. Filtering the measurement or the derivative term helps.
  • Over-aggressive tuning — pushing gains too high chases fast response at the cost of stability; the demos above make this easy to feel directly.

Where PID controllers show up in the real world

PID control is everywhere engineers need to hold a measured quantity at (or smoothly track) a target: cruise control and adaptive cruise control in cars, thermostats and HVAC systems, drone and quadcopter stabilization, 3D printer hotend temperature control, industrial process control (flow, pressure, level), robotics joint control, and audio/video sync loops. The math is the same everywhere — only the setpoint, measurement, and actuator change.
